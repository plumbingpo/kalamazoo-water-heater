Most gas water heaters in Kalamazoo, MI work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve shuts off the gas for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Kalamazoo Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. On every Kalamazoo call, our technicians test the thermocouple's millivolt output, inspect the pilot and burner flame, check gas pressure at the valve, and run a draft test at the draft hood to confirm exhaust is actually leaving the house. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

When to Call for Gas Water Heater Services

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Signs of backdrafting, such as soot, condensation, or an exhaust smell at the vent
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment
  • Rust-colored hot water or rust streaks on the tank
  • Pilot outages that keep coming back after a relight
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • Showers that go cold sooner than they did a year ago
  • No hot water at all, with the burner never firing

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Start with the hazards. Fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust laced with carbon monoxide come with every gas water heater. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and working through them by guesswork is slow and costly.

The value of calling a pro is certainty. We test instead of guessing, from thermocouple millivolt readings and burner flame checks to gas pressure at the valve and a draft test at the hood. Before we leave, every connection is leak-tested, the vent is confirmed to carry exhaust outside, and CO is checked. And because some gas heaters are not worth another repair, we give you a straight answer on repair versus replacement first, then handle either one with correct venting, gas piping, and the code items your installation requires.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Here are our recommendations:

  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Flush a few gallons from the drain valve periodically to control sediment
  • Replace a spent anode rod before the tank itself starts to rust
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Keep the air intake at the bottom of the heater free of lint and dust
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
